How Many Google Reviews Do I Actually Need?

There is no magic count. There is a comparison, a rate, and three ways to ruin the whole thing.

By Jack Wing, Wing Digital Published August 18, 2026 All posts →

The honest answer is that there is no number, and the businesses selling you one are guessing. What exists instead is a comparison and a rate. The comparison is against the three businesses currently sitting above you in the map pack. The rate is how many reviews you collect per month, from here on. Owners ask for a target because a target is easy to file away and forget about, and a rate is the thing that actually moves you.

So what number should I aim at?

Open Google, search your main service plus your city, and look at the three businesses in the map box. Write down what each one has: total reviews, average rating, and the date on the newest one. That is your bar, and it took ninety seconds to find. In a crowded Plano trade it might be a couple of hundred. In a specialised service two suburbs out it might be eleven. Anyone quoting you a universal figure has not looked at your map.

Reviews feed what Google calls prominence, one of the three factors named in its local ranking guidance, alongside relevance and distance. Prominence is Google's word for how well-known you are. It is comparative by nature, so your review count is only ever meaningful next to the people you are competing with.

Why the date on the newest review matters more than the total

This is the part that changes how you work. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey reports that 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses and 41% always do, up from 29% the year before. Buried under those headline figures is the finding that should reorganise your week: 74% seek reviews written in the last three months.

Three quarters of the people checking you out are not reading your reviews. They are reading your recent reviews, and everything older is furniture.

Sit with what that does to a business with sixty five-star reviews, the newest one from 2024. On paper it is a strong profile. To three quarters of the people looking at it, it is a company that was apparently excellent once. The competitor with nineteen reviews and four from this month looks like the one that is busy right now, and busy right now is what someone with a leaking roof is trying to establish.

So the useful question is not "how many do I have." It is "how many did I get in the last ninety days," and for most owners the answer is a number they do not like saying out loud.

What ruins a review page

Three mistakes, in descending order of how much damage they do:

  • Buying them or paying for them. Google's review policy is explicit that "offering incentives, like free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for customers to post reviews, change reviews, or remove negative reviews is considered fake and misleading content and is strictly prohibited." You are risking the profile your whole map ranking sits on, to buy something you could have asked for.
  • Asking only the customers you already know are happy. It feels harmless and it produces a page of flawless, weightless five stars that reads as bought even when it is not. A couple of four stars with real detail does more work than another perfect one.
  • Asking in bursts. Forty requests in one week after six quiet months produces a spike that helps nobody and looks exactly like what it looks like. Steady beats heroic.

How to actually get them, every week, without heroics

Willpower is not the answer, because willpower loses to a Friday. The answer is that the request stops depending on anyone remembering. When a job is marked complete, the request should already be written and waiting, addressed to that customer, with a link that drops them straight onto your review form. Someone glances at it, sends it, and moves on.

Two details make the difference between a system that runs and one that quietly dies. It has to be attached to the job finishing, not to a weekly reminder, because the reminder is what gets snoozed. And it has to go out while the work is still fresh in the customer's mind, which in practice means the same day. Ask on Thursday about a job you finished last month and you are asking someone to do homework.

The thing we will not do is send it for you unattended. A review request goes out in your name, to your customer, about a job we were not on. It gets drafted automatically and it waits for a person to read it and press send. That is a deliberate limit, not a missing feature.
